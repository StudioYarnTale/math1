🧮 What Is Mathematics?

A warm stillness rested on the sunny meadow. The air smelled of grass and daisies, bees were buzzing, and somewhere near the pond a frog splashed in the water. But right in the middle of the clearing, a serious discussion was taking place.

🐶Pippa the Dog and 🦊Liumy the Fox Cub were discussing a very important matter.
— Tomorrow is 🐷Peter the Piglet’s birthday! — said Pippa. — We must give him something wonderful.
— Of course! — agreed Liumy. — Let’s buy him a flute. I saw one in the toy shop for 9 coins.
— I think he would like a wind-up train, — objected Pippa. — He has dreamed about it for a long time.

Liumy thought for a moment.
— And how much does it cost?
— The train costs 13🪙 coins.
— I have 7🪙 coins, — said Liumy.

Pippa scratched her ear with her paw.
— And I had 20🪙… But I have already spent 9🪙 coins. She looked a little embarrassed and added:
— And I still need to buy pencils for 3 coins. And… actually, also a ribbon.
— A ribbon?
— Yes. I want to decorate my new dress. Auntie Polya said the ribbon costs one and a half coins per metre.

Liumy narrowed his eyes.
— And how many metres do you need?

Pippa sighed.
— That I do not know yet… I need to measure the dress first. She thought for a moment and said:
— But Auntie Polya says it is always better to take a little extra.
— Probably like from here to that bush.
— That is about three metres. — Liumy raised his ears.
— So we need to calculate whether we have enough coins for Peter’s present. He paused for a second… and suddenly brightened up and ran off.

A minute later he returned, dragging along a set of bright toy abacus beads.
— Here! Now everything will work!

Pippa carefully began moving the beads. Click… click…

But after a while, her ears slowly drooped.
— Liumy… — she said quietly. — Something isn’t working…

The little fox grew quiet as well. He felt rather embarrassed. Because… he did not know how to count either.

For some time they sat in silence.

Suddenly, along the path past the clearing ran 👧Anastasia, happily jumping rope.
— Anastasia! — called Pippa. — Help us!

The girl stopped.
— What happened?
— We cannot count the coins, — said Liumy.

Anastasia looked at the abacus in surprise.
— What… you really do not know how to count? Haven’t you learned mathematics?

Pippa and Liumy shook their heads shyly.
— What is mathematics? — asked Pippa.
— I heard that mathematics is boring and difficult, and that nobody really needs it now because computers do all the counting, — added Liumy.

Anastasia thought for a moment, then smiled.
— Then everything is clear.

She sat down on the grass beside them.
— Not at all! Mathematics is my favourite subject. It is easy and interesting! It is like a game, not scary numbers at all. And it is very important. It always has been and always will be.

Liumy leaned closer with interest.
— Why is that?

Anastasia picked up a stick and began drawing on the ground. She drew several small circles.
— For example, we need mathematics —
— to count coins,
— to find out whether we have enough money,
— to measure ribbon for a dress,
— to build a house,
— or even to understand how the stars move.

Pippa looked at the drawing in surprise.
— So… we cannot do without mathematics?
— Exactly! And mathematics is not only about counting — it is also about how the world works! About thinking and finding connections, — Anastasia said proudly.
— Thinking? — repeated Liumy. — Thinking how?
— Logically! — Anastasia declared solemnly, and then suddenly felt embarrassed. She herself did not really know what that meant. She had simply repeated her grandfather’s words. And now she would have to understand what it truly means to think logically.

Anastasia’s gaze fell again on the abacus. She gently moved one bead. Click. Then another. All ten beads. But how did it work? Anastasia frowned.
— Honestly… — she admitted, — I do not quite know how to use it either.

Pippa lifted her ears in surprise.
— Really?

Anastasia shrugged.
— Well, I am still learning. Nowadays everyone has calculators for counting — on phones and on computers. She thought for a moment and added:
— But that does not mean mathematics is not important. And your abacus is so beautiful. I would really like to understand how to use it.

Liumy brightened up.
— Maybe someone knows how the abacus works?

Pippa suddenly remembered.
— I know! — she wagged her tail happily.
— We should ask Phil the Gosling!
— Exactly! — exclaimed Liumy.

Anastasia looked at them with curiosity.
— And who is that?

Pippa explained:
— Phil loves books and knowledge very much. He is always studying something.

Liumy added:
— And he especially loves mathematics.

Anastasia immediately stood up.
— Then let’s go to Phil quickly!

She picked up the abacus.
— Let’s run. Maybe he will teach us how to use the abacus and solve problems.
— And then we will finally find out… — said Liumy.
— Whether we have enough money for Peter’s present! — finished Pippa.

And the three friends skipped along the path toward the pond.

What Kinds of Numbers Are There

Along the path to the pond walked three friends cheerfully: Anastasia, Liumy the Fox Cub, and Pippa the Dog. Anastasia carried a bright toy abacus in her hands, gently shaking them so the beads clicked musically against one another.
— Phil surely knows how to use them, — said Pippa.

Anastasia looked thoughtfully at the abacus.
— Actually, at school they told us that mathematics begins with something very simple, — she said.
— With NUMBERS.
— And what are numbers? — asked Liumy. — Are they like coins?

Anastasia bent down and began picking up pinecones from the ground, placing them into her hat while counting:
— 1 2 3 4 5.
— Numbers are used to count objects.

She placed the pinecones in a row.
— The numbers we use to count objects are called natural numbers.
— five pinecones…
— one ball…
— two apples…
— three coins…
— And what if I had not a single coin? — asked Pippa.

Anastasia smiled and wrote another number: 0
— This is ZERO.
— And what if I borrow 5 coins from Vasily Voldemarovich? — wondered Liumy.
— Then we will need negative numbers, — said Anastasia.

Anastasia drew a line and marked points on it: −5 −4 −3 −2 −1 0 1 2 3 Pointing at −5, she said:
— Look. This means that you did not have five coins, and you borrowed them.
— When you get your own coins, you will need to return those five — that is, subtract them.
— That is why such numbers are written with the sign «−» and are called negative numbers.
— And all these numbers together — negative numbers, zero, and positive numbers — are called integers.

Liumy looked surprised.
— So numbers can be smaller than zero?
— Yes, — said Anastasia. — Look, the height of this bush is 1 metre — that is a positive number. Then she pointed to a small hole in the ground.
— And here there is a hole twenty centimetres deep.
— If we take the ground surface as zero, everything above the ground is positive, and everything below it is negative.
— That means the depth of this hole can be written as −20 centimetres.


— And now the most interesting part. — Anastasia placed a point halfway between one and two.
— Now guess what number lives here?
— One and a half — 1+1/2! Just like my ribbon! One and a half coins per metre! — Pippa jumped with joy. — I could never understand how that worked. But now everything is clear!
— Correct, — said Anastasia.

She wrote: 1 + 1/2, or 1 whole 1/2.
— 1 and 1/2. A half, or 1/2, is called a fraction.
— So numbers are not only whole numbers? — asked Liumy.
— Of course, — said Anastasia confidently. She was very proud that she understood fractions. She had learned them quite recently and completed her school task better than anyone else, without a single mistake, receiving an “Excellent”.

Pippa suddenly remembered:
— Yesterday Auntie Polya gave me a slice of orange. Is that also a fraction?
— Yes, — said Anastasia.
— Auntie Polya divided the orange and gave one slice to you, to me, to Uncle Alex, to Liumy, kept one for herself and one for Peter the Piglet.

Liumy jumped up.
— I understand! She divided the orange into 6 slices!
— That means it is 1/6!
— Correct, — said Anastasia.
— And what if she had two oranges?
— That is something you can think about yourself, — said Anastasia, narrowing her eyes playfully ;-)

She pointed again at the line.
— This line is called the number line. On it we can mark any numbers: natural numbers, zero, integers, fractions.

She showed the numbers once more …−2 −1 0 1 2… Then she placed a point between two marks.
— Between whole numbers there live infinitely many fractions.

Pippa looked surprised.
— Infinitely many?
— Yes, — answered Anastasia. For example: — 1/2, 1/3, 3/4, 7/10.

Liumy frowned.
— And can all of them be written?
— Some numbers can be written as fractions. For example: 1/2, 2/3, — answered Anastasia, — but not all.

Pippa said:
— I know! My ball cost 4.7 coins. Is that also a fraction?
— Yes, — answered Anastasia.
— Such a fraction is called a decimal number, — she added.

But there are numbers that appear in geometry. Anastasia drew a square.
— This square has a side equal to 1.

She placed a stick along the side and marked a point.
— If we measure the diagonal of the square, we get a special number. The number √2.
— It cannot be written as a simple fraction or as a decimal fraction.
— Such numbers are called irrational numbers.

Pippa sighed. Now everything seemed too complicated.
— How many different numbers exist…
— Very many, — said Anastasia.
— And all of them live on the same number line. Even on this small segment there are so many numbers that it is impossible to count or even name them.
— But how can we learn mathematics if we cannot even count all of them? You said numbers are simple — but to me this seems very difficult, — said Pippa thoughtfully.
— Grandfather said that if there is something so large that it cannot be counted, it is called infinity! And this is how he drew it! ∞

Anastasia carefully drew the mysterious symbol in the sand with a stick.
— Beautiful, isn’t it?
— It is a mystery! — Liumy jumped with excitement. — I love everything mysterious!

At that moment Uncle Alex appeared on the path. He was carrying a measuring tape and a bundle of tools.
— Hello! — said Anastasia. — Uncle Alex, do you happen to have a calculator?

Uncle Alex scratched his head.
— A calculator? No, I do not have one. And I do not really need it.

Liumy looked surprised.
— Then how do you calculate?

Uncle Alex laughed.
— To be honest… I am not very good at calculating. At school I did not really like mathematics. It was difficult for me.

Pippa gasped.
— Really?
— Yes, — nodded Uncle Alex.

He pulled a long measuring tape from his pocket.
— But I do know how to measure.

He unrolled the tape.
— This is my main helper.
— If I need to build a fence — I measure.
— If I need to make a shelf — I measure.
— If I need to know how many bricks are required to repair a wall — I measure.

Anastasia smiled.
— But that is mathematics too.

Uncle Alex looked surprised.
— Really?
— Of course, — said Anastasia.
— Mathematics is not only about counting.
— It is also about measuring the world.

Liumy raised his ears.
— So mathematics lives not only in numbers…

Pippa finished:
— …but also in rulers, measuring tapes, and abacuses!

Anastasia shook the colourful abacus, and the beads rattled cheerfully.
— By the way… we still need to figure out how to use these.
— I think Phil definitely knows how they work.

And the friends continued on their way.

How the Abacus Counts

By the pond it was cool and quiet. The water gently lapped against the reeds, and dragonflies floated slowly above the smooth surface. Sitting on a large flat stone was Phil the Gosling. He adjusted the glasses at the very tip of his beak and carefully read a thick book.

When the friends came running to the shore, talking loudly and arguing, Phil raised his head. Anastasia quickly held out the bright toy abacus to him.

When Phil saw it, his face lit up with joy. His eyes sparkled, as if some wonderful and deep secret had suddenly opened before him.
— Ah, an abacus! What a wonderful abacus!

Phil gently pulled it closer and ran his wing over the colourful beads. Click… click…
— Listen — it sounds like music. You know, — he said softly, — people invented the abacus a very long time ago. Thousands of years ago.

Liumy opened his eyes wide in surprise.
— So long ago?

Phil nodded.
— Yes. In ancient cities people used small stones and clay balls to count grain, goods, and coins. Later came counting boards, abacuses, and after that — calculators and computers. He moved a few beads again. That is why mathematics is not just a set of rules. Phil looked at his friends and added mysteriously:
— Mathematics is the history of human thought.

Pippa gently touched a green bead.
— So… each bead is like a little secret?

Phil nodded.
— Exactly.
— Sometimes, to understand a large number… it is enough to move just one small bead.

He moved one bead. Click. Then another.
— Almost all of mathematics is hidden here.

Anastasia smiled.
— Then let’s try to understand it.

Phil carefully placed the abacus in front of his friends. The colourful beads tapped softly against each other.
— Look, — he said gently. — Let us begin with the simplest thing. Let us begin with ones. He touched the green row.
— Here live the ones. Each bead means 1 — like one coin.

He moved one bead. Click.
— One.

He moved a second bead. Click.
— Two.

Pippa nodded immediately.
— That is clear!

Phil smiled and pointed to the blue row above.
— And here live the tens.

He moved one blue bead.
— One ten is like ten coins. A blue bead equals 10 ones — that is, like 10 green beads.

Liumy brightened.
— Pippa, that means your twenty coins are two blue beads!
— Exactly, — nodded Phil.

Now he pointed to the yellow beads.
— These are hundreds. A yellow bead equals 100 ones — that is like 100 green beads or 10 blue ones.
— And the red ones are thousands. A red bead equals 1000 ones.

Pippa looked at the abacus with respect.
— Like a whole staircase made of numbers…
— Exactly, — said Phil. — Each step is ten times larger than the previous one.

Liumy looked carefully at the abacus. Suddenly he noticed the very bottom row — purple. He leaned closer.
— Wait… What is that?

Pippa also became curious.
— Yes! What are those beads?

Anastasia shrugged.
— They are below the ones…

Liumy thought.
— Maybe… those are the biggest numbers?

Pippa shook her head.
— No. They are below…

Anastasia suggested:
— Maybe… they are very small numbers?

Phil smiled even wider.
— Now that is closer to the truth.

He picked up a dry stick from the ground.
— Let us imagine this stick is like one green bead. He carefully broke it in half. Crack.
— Now we have two halves.

He picked up one piece.
— Each such half is called a fraction.

He wrote in the sand: 1/2
— We already know this! — Pippa and Liumy said at the same time. — Anastasia told us. He lifted the halves of the stick.
— Now let us divide even more precisely. Phil carefully divided each half into five smaller pieces. Now ten tiny pieces lay on the stone. He arranged them in a neat row.
— See?
— If one whole stick is divided into ten equal parts, each part is called one tenth. He wrote in the sand: 1/10

Anastasia nodded.
— Such fractions are often written differently. — She wrote beside it: 0.1
— This is a decimal fraction.
— We write it after a point. In Russian books it is often written with a comma: 0,1.

Liumy carefully watched the pieces.
— So… if we take five of these small parts… — Liumy placed five pieces together.
— We get a half. — He wrote: 0.5 = 1/2

Pippa laughed.
— So half a coin is five of these little pieces!

Phil nodded and pointed to the purple row on the abacus.
— That is exactly what the purple beads show.
— They represent tenths.

He moved one bead. Click.
— That is 0.1.

He moved five. Click-click-click-click-click.
— And that is 0.5.

Pippa now looked at the abacus very differently. It no longer seemed like a difficult puzzle. Instead — like a rainbow staircase of numbers, one that you could climb higher and higher. She carefully moved the beads.
— So… one and a half coins is one green bead and five purple ones?

Phil nodded happily.
— Exactly.

Pippa immediately jumped.
— Just like my ribbon! One metre of ribbon!
— Quite right, — said Phil.
— Then two metres of ribbon will be two green beads and 5 + 5 = 10 purple ones!
— Quite right, — said Phil.
— And three metres? — Pippa moved a green bead and thought. — We do not have any more purple ones…
— I know! — shouted Liumy. — You forgot that 10 purple beads can be replaced by one green bead!

Liumy moved one green bead and returned the purple ones.
— I understand! — Pippa exclaimed excitedly. — Wait, let me try myself. Three metres of ribbon will be four green beads and five purple ones! Right?
— Absolutely correct! Well done! — praised Phil.

Liumy jumped up.
— Then let’s start counting quickly!
— We need to find out whether we have enough coins for Peter’s present!

Phil smiled softly.
— Go ahead, count. You will succeed. Count slowly and carefully.
